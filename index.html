I’m a grad student in Computational Cognitive Science, working with Mick Bonner, studying representations in biological and artificial neural networks. My research investigates how brains and AI systems learn to internally represent information. More recently, my research has been focused on AI agents for neuroscientific discovery from large datasets (e.g. research internships at Microsoft Research and Sakana AI). My background is in CS, and I previously wrote code as a developer at Amazon. I’m excited about open-ended research, AI (and what aspects of work to offload to it without losing your core cognitive abilities like thinking or curiosity), traveling (experiences!), swim/bike training (david goggins 🙏🏻), and coffee ☕ I have also built the following tools:

Academia

Johns Hopkins University

PhD student

Computational Cognitive Science

2023 – present

  • Michael F. Bonner Assistant Professor of Cognitive Science

I study how brains and deep networks come to represent visual information, with a focus on computational models of visual processing. One line of work shows that vision models trained on extremely coarse objectives, as few as eight classes, still learn representations aligned with human judgments and visual cortex. I also build closed-loop AI systems for neuroscientific discovery, in which models propose, evaluate and refine hypotheses on neural data.

HHMI Janelia Research Campus

Research Engineer 2

Jan 2022 – Sep 2023

  • James Fitzgerald Associate Professor, Northwestern University

I worked directly with experimental neuroscientists to build connectome-constrained neural network models that can be fit end to end to recorded data. I used the framework to infer synaptic plasticity rules from learning dynamics. It recovered known rules and identified active forgetting in fly reward-learning data.

I implemented and benchmarked neural architecture search algorithms across vision, NLP, speech and detection. I built a unified interface for evaluating new search methods across datasets and search spaces, released as NAS-Bench-Suite in the open-source NASLib. With it we showed that conclusions drawn from a few benchmarks often do not carry over to others.

Gatsby Computational Neuroscience Unit

Research Resident

University College London

Jan 2019 – Jun 2020

  • Peter Latham Professor of Theoretical Neuroscience
  • Timothy Lillicrap Director of Research, Google DeepMind

I was the first Research Resident at Gatsby, working with Peter Latham and Tim Lillicrap on learning rules a brain could plausibly use. I implemented node perturbation and other local learning rules directly from their weight-update equations. I built efficient training code for per-weight updates and scaled it across GPUs, which made large experiments with these rules practical.

The work grew into a survey of deep learning for personality detection and several follow-up papers.
